# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Open on the actual exchange, status change, or named source-true development; the strongest ledes get to the news fast.
- Keep headline, subhead, and intro from re-saying the same point; each layer should add a new piece of the story.
- Use the sharpest sourced wrinkle as the hook when it is already in the digest, rather than padding with context.
- Tight, restrained framing helps short news items read as reporting instead of commentary.
- A modest explanation of why the update matters works best when it stays inside the confirmed facts.
- Strong execution can come from simple reporting: if every layer is specific and clean, no extra flourish is needed.
- Preserve a conservative causal chain; state the development without overclaiming its implications.
- [columnist] Stay tightly inside the source digest and let the article’s actual angle do the work; sourced details can carry the piece when they are used cleanly and without meta-language.
- [columnist] Specific supporting details strengthen the read when they are clearly tied to the reported exchange and do not drift into unsupported claims.
- [insider] Start with the confirmed transaction or roster move, then add only the consequence language the digest clearly supports.
- When the source gives only a small update, a narrow, disciplined treatment is better than forcing broader significance.
- When the digest already supplies a clear “play of camp” or similar hook, lean on that exact source-backed claim instead of general camp atmosphere.

## What to avoid
- Do not let the headline, subhead, and intro repeat the same framing; avoid lead-to-lead redundancy.
- Do not build the close around a fact or timeframe that the stronger digest does not clearly establish.
- Do not widen a narrow source fact into larger implications unless the digest actually supplies them.
- Do not let the intro drift into generalities when a more specific source-backed wrinkle is available.
- Do not build a lead out of paraphrase loops; each sentence should add something new.
- Do not overexplain a minor status update with analytical weight the source does not support.
- Do not mismatch the headline and body by making the headline more specific than the article underneath.
- Do not repeat the same availability/timeline point in headline, intro, and body without adding a fresh angle.
- Do not use generic filler or camp-language padding when the digest already gives a sharper hook.
- [columnist] Do not drift into unsupported claims or meta language; keep the read anchored in the actual jab-response or reported angle.
- [insider] Do not treat a transaction or reunion as proof of deeper roster-security or central-piece claims unless the source explicitly supports that read.
- [insider] Do not narrate sourcing or hedge around the digest when the story already has enough verified detail to state the move cleanly.
- Do not publish if the source digest is effectively empty or missing; a plausible story cannot be verified without facts (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Do not keep factual claims in play when the digest is missing or empty; that is a verification failure, not an execution tweak (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Do not anchor a story to a named player/team/context that the digest does not actually support; when the source is absent, the mismatch is a hard stop (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Do not use a broad “play of camp” claim when the source lacks concrete detail to prove it; unsupported camp-summary tone is a publish stop (source-side, not fixable in prose).
