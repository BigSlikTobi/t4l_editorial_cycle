# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Stay tightly grounded in the source and center the clearest fan-relevant development first.
- Use the strongest supported takeaway as the spine when several confirmed facts are available.
- Keep headline, lede, and body aligned on the same core development.
- Use specific, confirmed details like timing, deal terms, or context to make the note feel concrete.
- Let a clear team-specific hook drive the opening when it is directly supported.
- Use confirmed quotes or framing effectively, but only as support for the sourced point rather than as the whole story.
- Keep execution clean and restrained when the reporting is already strong; don’t add embellishment for its own sake.
- Make the story readable for the audience by emphasizing the practical implication without overstating it.
- A straightforward headline can work well when it states the core update plainly.
- Strong grounding plus precise source selection can make a conventional update feel high-value.
- For insider rewrites, a fresh second paragraph can restore wire-style pace after the lede lands.
- When the source clearly narrows the field, name the remaining options plainly instead of vague “next steps” language.

## What to avoid
- Don’t stretch limited source facts into broader implications than the material supports.
- Don’t recast a player’s stated timeline into a team-specific farewell or final-season claim unless the source explicitly says that.
- Don’t add unsupported scheduling, flexing, or other downstream implications more assertively than the source supports.
- Don’t let headline, lede, and body drift into slightly different angles; they should reinforce one another.
- Don’t bury the main supported hook under extra framing or explanatory padding.
- Don’t use a reported quote or context point as a springboard for unsourced roster-consequence claims.
- Don’t oversell significance in the headline if the body can only support a narrower claim.
- Don’t turn a clean sourced update into a bigger analytical thesis than the reporting justifies.
- Don’t rely on generic setup language when a direct, sourced statement will do.
- Don’t introduce cap, incentive, or depth-chart interpretation unless the source explicitly supports it.
- Don’t proceed as if thinly sourced details authorize extra inference; keep the note bound to what is actually confirmed.
- Don’t repeat the lede sentence in the opening paragraph; replace duplication with a new supporting detail.
- Don’t invent transaction specifics like dollar figures or awards when the source digest is empty or doesn’t confirm them (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t build a publishable note around contract, roster, or “keep him here” implications when the digest has no usable facts (source-side, not fixable in prose).
