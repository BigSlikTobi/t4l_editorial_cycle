# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Lead with the one verified development first, especially when the source is thin.
- Keep headline and lede aligned on the same sourced fact, and make each line add something new.
- Use a direct, restrained tone when the source only supports one clean takeaway.
- Let the confirmed quote, action, or event carry the hook instead of layering on extra interpretation.
- Stay inside the source’s exact scope; narrow inputs can still make strong stories when the framing is disciplined.
- Keep the lede concise and avoid front-loading meaning before the fact is stated.
- Strong sparse-source pieces stay at quote-level or fact-level confirmation and do not force extra color.
- Concrete status updates can work when every claim is explicitly grounded.
- [insider] Wire-style execution works when it is precise, concrete, and fully inside the digest.
- [columnist] A restrained columnist voice can still work when it stays tightly grounded and lets the confirmed detail do the work.
- [analyst] A usable hook is enough if the lede stays on the one sourced coaching signal and does not widen the frame.

## What to avoid
- Don’t let the headline and lede repeat the same claim without advancing with a new source-backed detail.
- Don’t add unsupported consequences, broader implications, or inferred fallout when the source confirms only one development.
- Don’t let the lede drift into summary language or generic interpretation that goes beyond the source.
- Don’t overbuild context around a source-light story; keep the framing tightly source-bound.
- Don’t infer motive, hidden details, or off-page specifics from a limited digest.
- Don’t use meta narration about what the source item does or does not spell out.
- Don’t bury the one confirmed takeaway under scene-setting, extrapolation, or speculative significance.
- Don’t repeat the same claim across headline, intro, and body without adding new source-backed detail; thin stories still need a distinct step forward.
- Don’t publish from an empty source digest or a factless headline hook: if the source is blank, the piece is not fixable in prose. (source-side, not fixable in prose)
- Don’t convert a source gap into certainty in the body or headline; missing support is a source-side problem, not a prose flourish issue. (source-side, not fixable in prose)
- [analyst] Don’t build a wider role-change or system-change story when the source only supports one player and one coaching signal.
- Don’t describe a vague hint as a concrete development unless the digest actually gives a concrete fact to anchor it.
- Don’t use source-meta phrasing such as “according to the digest” or “No additional details were included”; keep the prose in-story and plain.
- Don’t let a rewrite keep the right names but add unsupported commentary like a “public answer,” “offseason noise,” or who was “driving the conversation”; the fix is to narrow the claim, not decorate it.
- Don’t describe a bare digest as tied to a person or transaction unless the source actually substantiates that link; circular hedge language is still unsupported framing.
- Don’t state a transaction as fully completed if the source only supports a reported connection; match the hedge to the evidence.
- Don’t turn a clean sign-only update into a sourced-feeling mini feature by layering in extra context the digest doesn’t confirm.
- Don’t contradict the source headline with a different outcome or status.
- Don’t try to rescue an empty digest with added color, inferred detail, or invented context; if the source is blank, the piece is not fixable in prose. (source-side, not fixable in prose)
- Don’t stretch a stated intent into extra reporting or consequences; keep the claim at the level the source actually supports.
- Don’t let a headline and intro restate the same sourced claim without adding a second verified detail or a cleaner angle.
- Don’t build narrative around named-person rumor reaction when the digest itself is empty; that is a source failure, not a writer fix. (source-side, not fixable in prose)
