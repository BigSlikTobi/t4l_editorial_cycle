# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Open with the single confirmed fact and stay tightly inside the source.
- Lead with the concrete news point first; let sparse source material carry the piece.
- Keep headline and lede aligned on the same verified update, but vary the wording so they do not echo each other verbatim.
- Use restrained, source-faithful tone when the source supports only one narrow fact.
- Strong grounding is a major asset, especially when the story also has a clear human-interest or legacy angle.
- Tight, simple framing works when extra context would be speculative.
- If the source is thin, repeating the core fact once cleanly is better than reaching for unsupported context.
- When the source supports it, sharpen the intro around why the person or event matters to readers, not just the bare update.
- A concise note can work if it centers the confirmed update and avoids overbuilding around it.
- [columnist] A modest but concrete legacy or credential hook can be enough when it is fully grounded and the headline, intro, and body stay aligned.
- Keep camp-roundup framing factual and specific to the names actually supported by the digest.

## What to avoid
- Do not repeat the same status or fact in multiple forms without adding source-backed detail.
- Do not let the headline, lede, and first paragraph restate the same update in near-identical wording.
- Do not pad a straightforward update with broad color or generalized commentary.
- Do not add implications or evaluation claims unless the source directly supports them.
- Do not widen a single update into a bigger strategy, evaluation, or season story.
- Do not bury the verified news under unsupported framing or analysis language.
- Do not force context when the strongest story is simply the confirmed update.
- Do not let thin-source material be propped up by inference.
- Do not let the lede and body simply restate the same fact; if the source is sparse, make every sentence add a new grounded detail.
- Do not add consequence language like missed-time or monitoring claims unless the source confirms it.
- Do not add reflective filler or article-narration language that makes the piece sound self-conscious.
- Do not treat a narrow sourced note as a place to elaborate beyond the one concrete fact the source supports.
- [insider] Don’t meta-narrate the digest or source limits; if the source has no accessible facts, the piece should not assert a diagnosis or team linkage it cannot verify.
- Do not center unsupported named-player or camp-buzz claims when the digest does not supply facts for them.
- Do not drift into speculative depth-chart or camp-attention language unless it is tied directly to a fact already in the digest.
- Do not let roundup headlines promise more certainty than the source actually provides.
- [columnist] Treat “buzz” framing as unsupported if the digest offers no actual evidence behind it.
- [columnist] Avoid building a headline around roster chatter or player landing notes when the source is effectively empty; that is a source-side limitation, not a prose fix.
- [analyst] Keep the lede tightly on the actual decision point; do not add unsupported player-specific replacement claims or analysis when the digest is empty.
- [analyst] Don’t use a generic “decision looms” headline/lede pair that adds no concrete reason to care beyond the announcement itself.
- [analyst] Treat supporting names as context only when the digest mentions them; do not present them as confirmed candidates or implied conclusions.
- [insider] When the digest is effectively empty, avoid adding team-room context or receiver-room framing that the source doesn’t directly support.
- [insider] Make the intro add a sharper sourced update instead of simply rephrasing the headline.
- [insider] Cut repetitive sentence-level restatement; one clean pass at the confirmed development is stronger than three near-duplicates.
- [insider] If patience or status is the angle, keep it explicitly tied to the one verified injury/update rather than broad camp context.
- [insider] Do not assert unconfirmed status labels like rookie or “working through his rookie summer” unless the source explicitly says so.
- [insider] When the source supports only one fact, avoid building the story around identity details that are not in the digest.
- [analyst] Avoid asserting a looming quarterback decision, camp battle sharpening, or a player emerging as a defensive leader when the digest contains no verifiable facts; that is source-side, not fixable in prose.
- [analyst] Do not invent a concrete storyline from a missing or inaccessible source item; if the digest is empty, the article needs a real factual basis before it can be salvaged.
- [analyst] Do not frame a “replacement decision” or “left tackle replacement” as settled when the digest is empty; that is a source-side issue, not fixable in prose.
- [analyst] Avoid turning an inaccessible injury digest into a confirmed roster choice or positional outcome without closed-world facts.
- [insider] If the digest is empty, do not build the article around injury-patience narration or status drift; that is unsupported and source-side, not fixable in prose.
