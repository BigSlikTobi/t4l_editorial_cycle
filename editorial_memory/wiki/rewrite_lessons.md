# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Keep the lede tightly anchored to the single verified quote or update when the source is clean.
- Use a blunt, concrete quote as the hook when it is the strongest verified fact.
- Match headline and intro closely to the confirmed core fact so the piece feels grounded.
- Use only details explicitly present in the digest; a narrow frame is often stronger than speculative expansion.
- A restrained factual tone can work well when the source is simple and the update is straightforward.
- Give the reader at least one new verified detail beyond the headline instead of restating it in different words.
- Include concrete deal/contract terms when they are part of the confirmed package.
- [insider] Clean extension stories work best when they anchor the deal, duration, dollar figure, and confirmation source without overexplaining.
- [analyst] Keep the core verified move/comment centered, and let uncertainty stay implicit unless the source actually supplies details.
- Use a report-style hedge only when the source itself is incomplete and the article truly needs the attribution.
- Keep the story efficient: enough detail to justify publication, but no extra framing beyond the verified update.
- [analyst] When the digest is genuinely thin but still usable, keep claims strictly to the one clearly supported update and avoid layering on secondary implications.

## What to avoid
- Don’t widen a thin verified update into broader timing, implication, or organizational-impact language unless the source supports it.
- Don’t pad a simple coaching or quote-driven update with unsupported narrative flourish or explanation.
- Don’t let the headline promise a concrete move when the digest does not ground it clearly enough.
- Don’t repeat the same core fact in the headline, intro, and body; restating the lede makes the piece feel thin.
- Don’t use source-narration or report-framing filler to cover for a lack of verified facts.
- Don’t add timetable language unless it appears in the source digest.
- Don’t attach broader team-context claims to a sparse source summary just to make the item sound bigger.
- Don’t turn a straightforward update into analysis or speculation about consequences.
- Don’t build an article on an empty or near-empty digest; that is source-side, not fixable in prose (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t force a bigger angle onto a narrow update; keep the frame as tight as the source warrants.
- [analyst] Don’t assert unsupported second-track details when the digest only clearly supports the primary reaction or update.
- [analyst] Don’t use source-meta lines about what the report lacks; if uncertainty matters, express it briefly as a consequence of the available facts, not as commentary on the source.
- Don’t rely on repetitive execution that mainly restates the headline instead of delivering a publishable reason to tap.
- Don’t add tone-reading like relief, relief-through-the-process, or “moving on” narration unless the source explicitly supports that framing.
- Don’t publish a claim that cannot be verified inside the provided digests; if the source cluster is unusable or points to unrelated material, that is a source-side problem, not fixable in prose (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t mismatch the article’s factual claim with the source link/title; a grounding mismatch is a hard fail even if the headline sounds plausible.
- [analyst] Don’t name a specific figure or staff relationship when the digest is effectively empty; that is unsupported framing and the piece should be dismissed.
- [analyst] Don’t build an angle around a named pursuit/return scenario when the digests are empty; that’s a source-side grounding failure, not a rewriteable prose issue (source-side, not fixable in prose).
