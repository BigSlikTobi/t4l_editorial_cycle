# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Keep the story tightly inside the source’s exact quote or confirmed fact, especially on columnist pieces.
- Lead with the most concrete, source-backed stakes or decisions first; let broader stats or framing support the piece, not drive the lede.
- Make the headline and intro share the same factual hook without drifting into broader narrative.
- Use a direct, specific headline when the source gives a clean quote or clear event.
- Preserve a crisp factual sequence: exact statement first, minimal context second.
- Let one well-supported line carry the piece instead of expanding into bigger transition or “next chapter” framing.
- Strong named specifics can help, but only when every detail is directly supported by the source package.
- A tightly grounded quote-driven update can still read well when it avoids overexplaining the implications.
- [columnist] Keep the piece anchored to the speaker’s exact wording and the immediate, stated takeaway.
- [analyst] When the source offers a strong statistical or scheme hook, lead with that analytical frame and use the personnel move as proof, not vice versa.
- [analyst] Surface the most actionable decision point in the lede, then let later paragraphs add support instead of rehashing the setup.
- When the core fact is solid, keep the prose lean and let the concrete development do the work.

## What to avoid
- Don’t widen a tightly supported update into a larger transition or roster-change story unless the source clearly supports it.
- Don’t add “new chapter,” “next version,” or similar framing when the digest only gives a smaller, immediate quote.
- Don’t infer a broader strategic meaning from one coach quote unless the source actually spells it out.
- Don’t stack extra narrative around a clean source line; use the quote, not a storyline built beyond it.
- Don’t let the headline promise more context than the intro can verify.
- Don’t repeat the same setup in the headline, subhead, and intro; each element should add a distinct piece of information.
- Don’t let formation or stat framing crowd out sharper source-backed stakes that matter more to readers.
- Don’t imply roster implications, role shifts, or future direction that the digest does not directly state.
- Don’t overexplain a simple sourced remark with strategic language that isn’t in the source package.
- Don’t turn a source-supported comment into a “moving on” or “what comes next” article without evidence.
- [analyst] Don’t bury the most actionable team-building or scheme takeaway under a generic minicamp-battles frame; lead with the analytical hook, then use roster fights as evidence.
- Don’t repeat meta language about the source material when the rewrite can state the move and its immediate stake directly.
- Don’t proceed when the digest is too thin or internally noisy to support the named entity or cap-space detail; unsupported detail risk at rewrite stage is a publish blocker.
- Don’t paper over a grounding failure by leaning harder on headline wording if the source package itself doesn’t supply the needed facts.
- Don’t treat unrelated player mentions or noisy metadata as safe to carry into the story (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t add “according to reports” unless the source digest itself includes a concrete report detail.
- Don’t add speculation about how created cap space will be used; keep the rewrite to the confirmed restructure fact.
- Don’t use a thin digest to imply more than the restructure itself; source scarcity is a grounding problem, not a writing flourish problem.
- [insider] Don’t trust metadata that mixes unrelated names/entities into the story; if the digest is fact-thin or mismatched, stop instead of forcing a publishable rewrite (source-side, not fixable in prose).
