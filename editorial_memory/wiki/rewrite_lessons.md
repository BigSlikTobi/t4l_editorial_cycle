# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Open with the clearest verified status update when the source gives one; a concrete injury, availability, or role change can carry the piece.
- Lead with the most immediate, source-backed development rather than building up to it.
- Keep the lede and headline aligned on the same concrete event, but make sure each adds something distinct.
- Use short, direct status phrasing when the facts are specific and limited.
- Keep the story grounded in digest-confirmed details; a tight package can work even without big narrative framing.
- When the source supplies a clean sequence of events, present that sequence plainly instead of overexplaining it.
- For [insider], neutral watch-list reporting works best when it stays close to verified roster, injury, or game-state updates.
- If the piece has one strong factual hook, let that hook do the work rather than adding extra color.
- Clean execution often comes from stripping away meta language and keeping only the supported update.
- Tight packages are strongest when every sentence advances the verified status change or the most relevant sourced watch-list items.
- When the source is thin, a compact rebuild around only the verified facts can still work if the framing stays generic and cautious.
- Specific game context and a concise stat line can add useful grounding when they remain fully source-backed.

## What to avoid
- Do not attribute a quote, comment, or judgment to a person unless the digest explicitly supports that speaker role.
- Do not use report-meta phrasing like "according to the report" when a direct status update will do.
- Do not bury a clear injury, availability, or starter development under generic setup or scene-setting.
- Do not let the headline, intro, and subhead repeat the same status note without each contributing a distinct angle.
- Do not pad a narrow update with unsupported implications, aftermath, or broader narrative stakes.
- Do not overbuild a story around a single update by adding background that the source does not confirm.
- Do not swap in a convenient authority name if the source only identifies that name as the reporter, not the quoted source.
- Do not overstate certainty when the available source material only supports a straightforward factual update.
- Do not add unsupported medical detail beyond what the digests confirm.
- Do not introduce live-action, opener, or matchup framing that is not cleanly supported by the sourced watch-list story.
- Do not treat a source-clean but modest update as needing extra framing to be worthwhile.
- Do not invent team-specific framing or status implications when the digest is effectively empty; keep the lede at the level the source actually supports.
- Do not add extra embellishment once the key injury/status update is already clear and tightly sourced.
- Do not present a player watch list or availability angle when the digest is effectively empty; that is a source-side, not fixable in prose issue.
- Do not try to salvage an empty-digest story with rewrite polish alone; if the source lacks usable facts, the piece should not publish.
- For [insider], avoid speculative preseason lineup watch framing unless the digest actually supplies confirmed availability or lineup movement.
- Do not turn a no-facts digest into a named-player status roundup; unsupported lists are a hard stop, not a writing problem.
- Avoid implying there is meaningful breaking news when the source content does not verify any update at all.
- Do not use a roster-watch headline if the underlying source cannot support any specific roster, injury, or lineup claim.
