# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Lead with the concrete camp/practice change immediately; the first sentence should tell readers what is actually known.
- Keep headline and lede pointed at the same verified core claim so the article feels grounded.
- Use tight, digest-faithful framing when the file supports a narrow update better than a broad angle.
- Preserve specific confirmed details and watch-list items when they are actually supported by the source set.
- Stay centered on the named subject or event instead of drifting into unrelated package material.
- Clean, direct wording works well when the angle is simple and the payload is specific.
- Anchor the opening to the main fact before adding secondary details or sequence notes.
- Give readers the practical consequence of the update when it is explicitly supported, such as role changes or camp impact.
- Pair a personal/story element with a concrete status update when both are source-backed; that can make a rehab or clearance story feel fan-useful without overreaching.
- [analyst] Lead with the concrete practice/workload change and the actual competition it affects; a precise schedule note plus a supported QB-battle angle can carry the piece.
- [analyst] Use source-backed stakes or quotes to anchor the update, not to pad it.

## What to avoid
- Do not overstate or invent details beyond what the digest supports.
- Do not use source-meta or report-style phrasing that pulls the copy outside the digest.
- Do not let the lede merely restate the headline; it should add a new layer of information.
- Do not broaden a narrow update into unrelated context coverage.
- Do not let the headline promise one clean angle while the body drifts into another.
- Do not mix unrelated source material into the story when it does not serve the core angle.
- Do not add interpretation beyond the confirmed facts and timeline.
- Do not overcomplicate a clean update with extra narrative baggage.
- Do not repeat the same status note in the headline, lede and subhead; say it once, cleanly, then move on.
- Do not let source-narration filler dilute a breaking-news voice.
- [analyst] Cut unsupported side claims that are not needed for the core development, even if they are colorful or adjacent.
- [analyst] Avoid repetitive ledes/package wording; make the opening advance the story with fresh, source-backed detail.
- [source-side, not fixable in prose] If the file arrives with unsupported claims or mismatched package material, the fix may require source cleanup, not just rewriting.
- [source-side, not fixable in prose] If the source leans on unsupported narration, tighten the prose, but recognize the underlying claim set may still need cleanup.
- [source-side, not fixable in prose] If the digest is effectively empty or missing its summary facts, there may be nothing solid to salvage in prose.
- Do not add emotional or commemorative framing unless the source explicitly supports it; unsupported “remembered” language reads as overreach.
- Do not force a story when the available material cannot sustain one; empty-source dismissals should trigger source cleanup, not a speculative rewrite.
- Do not blur a status update into drama; if the source supports a limited/partial availability note, keep the language factual and operational rather than sensational.
- Do not mix certainty and hedging in the same paragraph when the source support is weak; either stay fully sourced or strip the claim back.
- [source-side, not fixable in prose] If the source is missing or provides no key facts, do not carry forward a claimed camp/injury update as verified; dismiss as unsupported rather than rewriting around it.
- [source-side, not fixable in prose] If the digest is effectively empty, the correct move is to stop rather than invent a publishable version of the update.
