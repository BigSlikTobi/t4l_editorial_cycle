# Editorial Style Wiki

## What works (keep doing)
- Keep the headline, intro, and body locked onto one clear development; alignment across the package is a strength.
- A narrow, concrete update can carry the piece when the fact pattern is clean and specific.
- Tight factual discipline is a virtue: stay inside the source material from start to finish.
- Direct, specific writing with no unsupported embellishment remains a strong default.
- A restrained, fact-first lede works well when the story is about one confirmed point rather than a broader trend.
- Preserve a clear line from headline to body so the reader immediately understands the core update.
- Concise status or practice notes can still be publishable if they answer one concrete question well.
- Strong, named details can add bite when they stay tightly tied to the central point.
- [analyst] Lead with the concrete roster/contract angle when it is the cleanest, most verifiable hook.
- [insider] Lead with the blunt, confirmed quote or warning, then anchor it quickly to the most supportable context.
- [columnist] A single sourced opinion or comparison can work if it is framed as one clear takeaway and not stretched into broader certainty.
- [columnist] Keep the lede lean when the source offers one usable point; let the quote and comparison do the work.
- [columnist] A concrete, fan-facing hook can work when every part of the package stays inside the supported source material.

## What to avoid
- Don’t widen a small update into broader team-level speculation that the source does not support.
- Don’t pad a thin fact with generic “why it matters” language if the source digest does not supply it.
- Don’t repeat the headline verbatim in the subhead or intro; avoid redundant phrasing.
- Don’t let filler language create facts or momentum the source never supplied.
- Don’t overstate certainty or imply consequences the source does not verify.
- Don’t bury the strongest factual takeaway under broad summary language or meta commentary.
- Don’t force a broader frame when the real story is a single confirmed development.
- Don’t present source bundles with muddy fit as if they were fully clean; keep the package disciplined.
- Don’t repeat the same premise across headline, intro, and body without adding new source-backed detail.
- Don’t lean on unsupported certainty about a projected lineup or named player resolution when the source is only a title-level hint.
- Avoid meta-feeling lines that talk around the payoff instead of delivering it.
- [analyst] Don’t stack headline, subhead, and intro with the same setup; advance the piece instead of restating the angle.
- [analyst] Don’t let analyst copy stay generic or repetitive when the source supports a specific roster watch or concrete consequence.
- [analyst] Don’t lean on unsupported fit/trait claims when the source-backed role, stats, and contract details are already strong enough.
- [analyst] Don’t introduce unsupported names, roles, or outcome claims; if a name appears in the source, mention it only at the level the source supports.
- [insider] Don’t add unsupported contract, injury, or status specifics beyond what the digest establishes.
- [insider] Don’t let the lede become repetitive by restating the same setup instead of advancing to the anchored context.
- [insider] Don’t build a polished narrative around details that are not established in the provided digests (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- [insider] Don’t phrase “trending toward” or similar movement language as confirmed progress unless the digest explicitly supports that level of certainty.
- [columnist] Don’t turn one quoted opinion into a wider factual claim unless the digest explicitly supplies the support.
- [columnist] Don’t use source-narration or reader-walking language when the stronger move is to state the one grounded takeaway directly.
- Don’t publish when the source digests are effectively empty or the story depends on unsupported framing; that’s a source-side issue, not fixable in prose (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t use source-narration/meta phrasing like “the reported direction,” “reported shift,” or “No further specifics were included” when the digest itself has no usable facts; that’s a source-side, not fixable in prose problem (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t introduce extra named entities or side mentions that aren’t grounded in the provided source material; they read as unsupported framing (source-side, not fixable in prose).
- Don’t lean on broad theme-driven interpretation when the digest has no usable facts to support it; that reads as unsupported reporting, not just weaker style.
- Don’t use source-meta phrasing like “the source also names” to bridge gaps; if the digest is thin, acknowledge that the story is not yet reportable rather than narrating the source.
- Don’t add claims about tone, standards, or a “reset” unless the digest explicitly backs them; those details quickly become invention when the source is bare.
- Don’t turn a single health update or availability note into broad commentary about the team picture unless the sourced detail genuinely supports that expansion.
- Don’t use generic filler around a one-point update when the stronger move is to sharpen the lede and cut repetition.
- Don’t restate the same availability note in multiple sentences; each sentence should advance with a new sourced detail or angle.
- Don’t lean on broad line-room commentary when the digest only supports the named player’s update.
